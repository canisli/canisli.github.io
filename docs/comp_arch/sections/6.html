6.3. Computer

We use AND/OR/NOT to compute the arithmetic logic unti. Flip-flops for registers, RAMs, and counters. And we’re done!

The stored program concept
“Early computers were wired to do one specific task. The breakthrough insight (Turing, von Neumann,  1940s) was: store the instructions in memory too, not just the data. Then the computer can read its own instructions out of RAM and execute arbitrary programs without rewiring anything. This is why your computer can run a browser and a game and a compiler — same hardware, different data in memory.”

The fetch-decode-execute cycle
A CPU just does this in a loop, billions of times per second:

Fetch — read the next instruction from memory (using the program counter register) Decode — figure out what the instruction means (is it an add? a memory load? a jump?) Execute — route signals through the ALU or memory accordingly, update registers

That’s the entire runtime of every program you’ve ever used.

Claude explanation

The energy cost comes from something more subtle: capacitance.

Every wire has capacitance Any two conductors near each other form a capacitor — they can store a small amount of charge. In a chip, every wire and transistor gate is surrounded by other wires and silicon, so every node in the circuit has a tiny capacitance whether you want it or not.

To change a wire from 0 to 1, you have to charge that capacitance. That requires moving electrons, which requires current, which costs energy. When it goes from 1 back to 0, that charge dumps to ground — also dissipated as heat.

So every 0→1→0 transition burns a little energy, proportional to the capacitance of that wire.

Why steady state is nearly free If a transistor is just sitting at 0 or 1 and not switching, the capacitors are already charged (or discharged) and nothing is moving. Essentially zero current flows, essentially zero power consumed. This is the key advantage of CMOS — the transistor technology used in modern chips — over older designs where current flowed continuously.

Why this makes clock gating valuable The clock wire is the most active wire in the entire chip — it switches every single cycle, and it fans out to every flip-flop everywhere. It’s a huge capacitance being driven billions of times per second. Clock gating — stopping the clock to idle sections — can cut a significant fraction of total chip power just by reducing those transitions.

This is why your phone’s CPU spends most of its time with large parts of itself clock-gated off, only waking them when needed.
