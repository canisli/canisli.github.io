8. Scattering in 3D

8.1. Scattering Amplitudes

Definition 10. A collision between particles is called scattering if the initial and final state particles are the same. The scattering is elastic if none of the initial states of the particles change in the collision.

Assumptions:

  • No spin
  • Nonrelativistic
  • Interaction potential is translationally invariant, i.e. 𝑉(𝐫𝟏,𝐫𝟐)=𝑉(𝐫1−𝐫2).
  • We will assume the potential is either finite with range 𝑎 or decays faster than 1/𝑟.

Let 𝑚 denote the reduced mass of the system. The Hamiltonian is

𝐻=𝐩̂22𝑚+𝑉(𝐫). (91)

Far away the solutions take the form

Ψ(𝐫,𝑡)=𝜓(𝐫)𝑒−𝑖𝐸𝑡/ℏ (92)

where 𝐸=(ℏ𝑘)2/(2𝑚)>0, and 𝜓(𝐫) solves the TISE:

[−ℎ22𝑚∇2+𝑉(𝐫)]𝜓(𝐫)=𝐸𝜓(𝐫) (93)

How do we enforce scattering solutions? Recall in 1D, we had an incident wave 𝜑 plus a scattering wave 𝜓𝑠, including both reflected and transmitted wave for |𝑥|≫𝑎. In 3D, our ansatz for large 𝑟 is an incident wave plus a radial scattering wave:

𝜓(𝐫)=𝜑(𝑟)+𝜓𝑠(𝑟)=𝑒𝑖𝑘𝑧+𝑓𝑘(𝜃,𝜙)𝑒𝑖𝑘𝑟𝑟,𝑟≫𝑎 (94)

We call 𝑓𝑘(𝜃,𝜙) the scattering amplitude. Note that in zero potential 𝑒𝑖𝑘𝑟/𝑟 solves the Schrodinger equation, but 𝜓𝑠 does not. This approaches an exact solution of Equation 93 for 𝑟→∞.

Before deriving 𝑓𝑘(𝜃,𝜙), let’s consider observables.

  • Classically, we imagine the differential cross section d𝜎dΩ as the area of the portion of the beam that will scatter in the direction of a given solid angle dΩ (8.223). More generally (in classical and quantum), we define d𝜎 as the area that would remove from the incident beam the particles scattered into the solid angle dΩ. We have

    d𝜎=# particles scattered per unit time intodΩflux of incident particles per unit time. (95)

From Equation 94, the denominator is given by 𝐽=ℏ𝑘/𝑚. The numerator is 𝐉𝐬⋅𝑟2dΩ. We obtain

d𝜎dΩ=|𝑓𝑘(𝜃,𝜙)|2. (96)

The total cross section 𝜎=∫d𝜎 represents the area that removes from the incoming beam a flux of particles equal to the flux of the scattered particles. This does not concern the location of the actual area in the beam.

8.2. Central potential

Now let’s solve Equation 93 for a central potential. We have azimuthal symmetry so 𝜙 drops from 𝑓𝑘.

First, we rewrite the plane wave in the 𝑧-direction via spherical coordinates:

    𝜎𝑙≤4𝜋𝑘2(2𝑙+1) (107)

8.3. Computing the Phase Shift

Now that we have this nice ansatz for each partial wave, how do we go about computing each 𝛿𝑙? Here is the strategy:

  1. Recall solutions restricted to a single partial wave take the form

    𝜓(𝐫)𝑙=(𝐴𝑙𝑗𝑙(𝑘𝑟)+𝐵𝑙𝑛𝑙(𝑘𝑟))𝑌𝑙0(𝜃),𝑟>𝑎. (110)
  2. Match the solutions inside the potential to the partial waves to solve for the ratio 𝐵𝑙𝐴𝑙=−tan𝛿𝑙.
  3. Use the relation tan𝛿𝑙=−𝐵𝑙/𝐴𝑙

Example 6 (Hard sphere). For small 𝑘𝑎, we have 𝜎→4𝜋𝑎2.
