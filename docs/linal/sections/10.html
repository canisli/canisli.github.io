[⭐️ 5.27] Over ℂ, every operator has an upper triangular matrix

Prove via induction. Obviously true for dim𝑉=1. Suppose dim𝑉>1, and the theorem holds for all complex vector spaces with dimension less than that of 𝑉.

Let 𝜆 be an eigenvalue of 𝑇, whose existence is guaranteed by 5.21. Let 𝑈=range(𝑇−𝜆𝐼). By FTOLM, dim𝑈<dim𝑉. In addition, it’s not difficult to show that 𝑈 is invariant under 𝑇. Thus we can construct a basis 𝑢1,…,𝑢𝑚 of 𝑈 of which 𝑇 has a triangular matrix with respect to. Extend this basis of 𝑈 to a basis of 𝑉, 𝑢1,…,𝑢𝑚,𝑣1,…,𝑣𝑛. For each 𝑣𝑗, we have 𝑇𝑣𝑗=(𝑇−𝜆𝐼)𝑣𝑗+𝜆𝑣𝑗. Thus 𝑇𝑣𝑗∈𝑣𝑗+𝑈), meaning that the matrix of 𝑇 wrt to this new basis is upper triangular. □

[7.24, 7.29] Spectral theorems

Complex spectral theorem

  • For normal operators ‖𝑇𝑣‖=‖𝑇∗𝑣‖.
  • Proof that (𝑎)→(𝑐). Use Schur’s lemma and argue the nondiagonal entries of the upper triangular matrix must be zero.

Real spectral theorem:

  • Suppose 𝑈 is invariant under 𝑇 and 𝑇 is self-adjoint. Then 𝑈⟂ is also invariant under 𝑇.
  • Incorrect proof that (𝑎)→(𝑐). Use Gram-Schmidt to find an orthonormal basis of 𝑉. Since 𝑇 is self-adjoint the matrix of 𝑇 with respect to this basis must equal its conjugate transpose, and thus ℳ︀(𝑇) is a diagonal matrix. This implies the orthonormal basis is of eigenvectors. THIS proof doesn’t work because it only proves that ℳ︀(𝑇) is symmetric, not diagonal.
