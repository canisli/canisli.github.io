5. Operators on inner product spaces

All vector spaces in this chapter are finite-dimensional inner product spaces.

5.1. Adjoint and Normal

Suppose 𝑇∈ℒ︀(𝑉,𝑊). Consider the linear functional given by 𝜑(𝑣)=⟨𝑇𝑣,𝑤⟩ for some 𝑤∈𝑊. By the Riesz representation theorem, there is a unique 𝑤1∈𝑊 such that 𝜑(𝑣)=⟨𝑇𝑣,𝑤⟩=⟨𝑣,𝑤1⟩. Let 𝑤1=𝑇∗𝑤. This motivates the definition of function 𝑇∗:𝑊→𝑉, called the adjoint of 𝑇, such that

⟨𝑇𝑣,𝑤⟩=⟨𝑣,𝑇∗𝑤⟩for all𝑣∈𝑉,𝑤∈𝑊. (20)
  • [7.5] It is not difficult to see that 𝑇∗ is a linear map.
  • [7.10] Suppose 𝑒1,…,𝑒𝑛 and 𝑓1,…𝑓𝑚 are orthonormal bases of 𝑉 and 𝑊, respectively. Then ℳ︀(𝑇,(𝑒1,…,𝑒𝑛),(𝑓1,…,𝑓𝑚)) is the conjugate transpose of ℳ︀(𝑇∗,(𝑓1,…,𝑓𝑚),(𝑒1,…,𝑒𝑛))
  • [7.14*️] Suppose 𝑉 is a complex inner product space and 𝑇∈ℒ︀(𝑉).
    ⟨𝑇𝑣,𝑣⟩ for all 𝑣∈𝑉 ⟹𝑇=0

Self-adjoint operators are also called Hermitian. Here are some properties:

  • [7.12] 𝑇 is self adjoint ⟹ every eigenvalue of 𝑇 is real.

An operator is normal if it commutes with its adjoint. Every self-adjoint operator is obviously normal.

  • [7.20] 𝑇 is normal ⟺ ‖𝑇𝑣‖=‖𝑇∗𝑣‖ for all 𝑣∈𝑉.
    • A stronger equality is that ⟨𝑇𝑣,𝑇𝑤⟩=⟨𝑇∗𝑣,𝑇∗𝑤⟩ for all 𝑣,𝑤∈𝑉.
  • [7.21] 𝑇 is normal, 𝑇𝑣=𝜆𝑣 ⟹ 𝑇∗𝑣=𝜆‾𝑣. Thus, 𝑇 and 𝑇∗ have the same eigenvectors.
  • [7.22] 𝑇 is normal ⟹ ecigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.

Theorem 6 (Spectral Theorems). For a real finite-dimensional vector space 𝑉 and 𝑇∈ℒ︀(𝑉), the following statements are equivalent:

  1. 𝑇 is self-adjoint.
  2. 𝑉 has an orthonormal basis consisting of eigenvectors of 𝑇.
  3. 𝑇 has a diagonal matrix with respect to some orthonormal basis of 𝑉.

The complex version is analogous, except replace (1) with 𝑇 is normal.

5.3. Positive operators and isometries

An operator 𝑇 is called positive if 𝑇 is self-adjoint and ⟨𝑇𝑣,𝑣⟩≥0 for all 𝑣∈𝑉.

  • If 𝑉 is complex, the requirement that 𝑇 is self-adjoint can be dropped (by 7.15).
  • [7.35] 𝑇 is positive ⟺ All the eigenvalues of 𝑇 are nonnegative.

An operator 𝑆∈ℒ︀(𝑉) is called an isometry if ‖𝑆𝑣‖=‖𝑣‖ for all 𝑣∈𝑉, i.e. 𝑆 preserves norms. ℳ︀(𝑆) and 𝑆 can be called unitary and orthogonal as well.

  • 𝑆 is an isometry ⟺ ⟨𝑆𝑢,𝑆𝑣⟩=⟨𝑢,𝑣⟩ for all 𝑢,𝑣∈𝑉.
  • 𝑆 is an isometry ⟺𝑆𝑆∗=𝑆∗𝑆=𝐼 (Recall 𝑇∗ denotes adjoint)
  • 𝑆 is an isometry ⟹ detℳ︀(𝑆)=1. The converse has easy counterexamples.

5.5. Single value decomposition

Motivation: A unitarily diagonalizable matrix 𝐴 can be expressed as

𝐴=∑𝑖𝜆𝑖𝑒𝑖⊗𝑒𝑖∗({𝑒𝑖}orthonormal eigenbasis). (22)

We are interested in whether we can get a similar for rectangular matrices based on orthonormal bases. In particular, If we have an 𝑚×𝑛 matrix 𝑀, do we have a similar decomposition3 𝑀=∑𝑖𝑠𝑖𝑢𝑖⊗𝑣𝑖∗?

Lemma 1.
  1. For any symmetric matrix 𝑀, rank𝑀 equals the number of nonzero eigenvalues of 𝑀.
  2. rank𝐴𝖳𝐴=rank𝐴
Proof
  1. Switch basis to the diagonal matrix and this becomes obvious.
  2. By rank-nulltiy, it suffices to show that ker𝐴𝖳𝐴=ker𝐴. ker𝐴⊂ker𝐴𝖳𝐴 is obvious. Suppose 𝐴𝖳𝐴𝑥=0. Left multiply both sides by 𝑥𝖳 to obtain 𝐴𝑥=0. Hence ker𝐴𝖳𝐴⊂ker𝐴.

□

⟨𝑢𝑖,𝑢𝑓⟩=1𝑠𝑖𝑠𝑗𝑣𝑖𝐴𝖳𝐴𝑣𝑗=𝑠𝑗𝑠𝑖⟨𝑣𝑖,𝑣𝑗⟩=𝛿𝑖𝑗 (25)

Geometrically, the SVD corresponds to rotating/reflecting into some ONB, stretch along axes, and then rotating/reflecting into the output ONB.
