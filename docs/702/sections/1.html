1. Representation theory of finite groups

1.1. Motivation and Definition

  • A finite group can be described in terms of its presentation: a set of generators and relations among those generators. For example,

    𝐷3=⟨𝑟,𝑠|𝑟3=𝑠2=𝑒,𝑠𝑟𝑠−1=𝑟2⟩𝑆3=⟨𝑠1,𝑠2|𝑠12=𝑠22=(𝑠1𝑠2)3=𝑒⟩. (1)
    From these presentations it is not obvious that 𝐷3≅𝑆3. But from looking at the actions (permuting vertices of a triangle) it becomes visualizable.
  • Eventually actions become unwieldy and we want more structure. Describing them as linear actions lets us have notions of decomposition, eigenvalue, orthogonality. Hence we obtain a systematic framework—unavailable at the level of presentations or set actions alone—that we can apply to Fourier analysis and equivariant neural networks.

  • The idea of R.T. is to realize groups as groups of linear operations. For example we have the standard representation of 𝐷3:

    𝑟↔rotation by2𝜋/3𝑠↔reflection about𝑥 (2)
    This defines a homomorphism 𝜌st:𝐷3→GL(ℝ2).

Definition 1. A complex representation of 𝐺 on 𝑉 is a homomorphism

𝜌:𝐺→GL(𝑉). (3)

The dimension of a representation (𝜌,𝑉) is said to be dim𝑉

  • Examples:
    1. 𝜌triv:𝐺→GL(ℂ)≅ℂ×𝑔↦1.
    2. 𝜌pm:𝑆𝑛→GL𝑛(ℂ).
    3. 𝜌sign:𝑆𝑛→ℂ×

    4. (Regular representation) Let 𝑉 be a vector space of dimension |𝐺| with basis elements 𝑒𝑔 for all 𝑔∈𝐺.

      𝜌reg:𝐺→GL(𝑉)𝜌reg(ℎ)(𝑒𝑔)=𝑒ℎ𝑔 (4)
      Each 𝜌reg(𝑔) is simply a permutation matrix.

Remarks.

  • Sometimes we will abuse notation and let 𝑔𝑣≡𝜌(𝑔)𝑣.
  • A group representation can be viewed as a group action on 𝑉 (homomorphism from 𝐺 to Perm𝑉) that is also linear.

Definition 2 (Equivariance). Suppose (𝜌,𝑉) and (𝜌′,𝑉′) are two representations of 𝐺. A linear map 𝑇:𝑉→𝑉′ is a 𝐺-homomorphism if it commutes with all elements of 𝐺:

𝑇(𝜌(𝑔)𝑣)=𝜌(𝑔)𝑇𝑣for all𝑔∈𝐺,𝑣∈𝑉. (5)

We also say 𝑇 is equivariant to 𝐺.

Example 1. Equivariance is of interest in machine learning for tasks where we want the model output to transform in the same way as its input. E.g. for pixel classification, if we reflect the input image the output should be also reflected. See ml.typ for more.

𝑇 is a 𝐺-isomorphism if 𝑇 is invertible, so that we can write

𝜌′(𝑔)=𝑇−1𝜌(𝑔)𝑇 (6)

One goal of representation theory is to find all representations of a group up to isomorphism.

1.2. Irreps

Definition 3. (𝜌,𝑉) is irreducible if the only 𝐺-invariant subspaces of 𝑉 are {0} and itself.

Example: Any 1-dimensional representation is irreducible. Kernel and image of an equivariant map are 𝐺-invariant.

Lemma 1 (Schur's Lemma). Suppose (𝜌,𝑉) and (𝜌′,𝑉′) are irreducible representations of 𝐺.

  1. Suppose 𝑇∈Hom𝐺(𝑉𝜌,𝑉𝜌′′). Then either 𝑇=0 or 𝑇 is a 𝐺-isomorphism.
  2. Suppose 𝑇∈End𝐺(𝑉𝜌). Then 𝑇 is a scalar operator, i.e. dimEnd𝐺(𝑉𝜌)=1
Proof
  1. Casework on whether ker𝑇 is trivial, and apply the definition of irreducibility.
  2. Take an eigenvalue 𝜆 of 𝑇. Apply (1) to show that 𝑇−𝜆𝐼=0.

□

Later we will see that the following converse of Schur’s lemma holds from Maschke’s theorem:

Lemma 2 (Schur's Lemma, Converse). Suppose 𝜌 is reducible. Then dimEnd𝐺(𝑉𝜌)>1

Remark. The existence of an equivariant map implies that the two irreps are isomorphic.

Lemma 3. Any irreducible representation of a finite group 𝐺 is of dimension ≤|𝐺|.
Proof
Let 𝑣∈𝑉 by nonzero and consider the subspace 𝑊=span({𝑔𝑣|𝑔∈𝐺}). By construction, 𝑊 is 𝐺-invariant and dim𝑊≤|𝐺|. We must have 𝑉=𝑊 by the definition of irreducibility, implying dim𝑉≤|𝐺|

□

1.3. Complete Reducibility

Consider a 𝐺-invariant subspace 𝑊⊂𝑉. Since 𝜌(𝑔) is a linear map, we have 𝜌(𝑔)𝑊=𝑊, and we can consider the subrepresentation of 𝜌(𝑔) restricted to 𝑊. Notably, if 𝑉 is the direct sum of two 𝐺-invariant subspaces, the matrix of 𝜌(𝑔) with respect to their basis is block diagonal, and we can define a direct sum of representations:

(𝜌,𝑉)=(𝜌|𝑊1,𝑊1)⊕(𝜌|𝑊2,𝑊2). (7)

Can all reducible representations can be decomposed like this (called completely reducible)? Yes for finite groups (Maschke’s theorem) but additional assumptions on 𝐺 are required for infinite group.

Counterexample for infinite groups

Let 𝐺=(ℝ,+), and define a 2D representation 𝜌:𝐺→GL2(ℝ) by

𝜌(𝑡)=(1𝑡01) (8)
This doesn’t contradict Peter-Weyl since 𝐺 is not compact.
Theorem 1 (Maschke's Theorem). Every representation of a finite group 𝐺 on 𝑉 over a field 𝔽 with characteristic not dividing the order of 𝐺 is a direct sum of irreducible representations.

Corollary: Every finite group has an irreducible representation.

Before proving Maschke’s Theorem, we introduce the notation of unitary representations.

Let (,) be a positive definite Hermitian form on 𝑉. Recall that 𝑇∈GL(𝑉) is a unitary operator if

(𝑇𝑣,𝑇𝑣′)=(𝑣,𝑣′)for all𝑣,𝑣′∈𝑉. (9)

Definition 4. We say (𝜌,𝑉) is a unitary representation if 𝑉 has a positive definite Hermitian form (,) such that

(𝑔𝑣,𝑔𝑣′)=(𝑣,𝑣′)for all𝑣,𝑣′∈𝑉. (10)

Equivalently, there exists a change of basis such that 𝜌(𝑔) is a unitary matrix1 for all 𝑔.

Proof of equivalence

Suppose there exists such a unitary form. Then we have 𝜌(𝐺)†𝐻𝜌(𝑔)=𝐻 for some Hermitiain positive-definite matrix 𝐻. Then take the Cholesky decomposition 𝑆†𝑆, so that in the new basis 𝑣′=𝑆𝑣, the representation becomes 𝜌′(𝑔)=𝑆𝜌(𝑔)𝑆−1, and 𝜌′(𝑔)†𝜌(𝑔)

For the reverse proof, we can construct 𝐻 using the change of basis. □

As a consequence of the second definition, each 𝜌(𝑔) can be diagonalized according to some basis that depends on 𝑔.

Example: Let 𝐹(𝐺)={𝑓:𝐺→ℂ} be the vector space of complex valued functions on 𝐺. Define

(𝜆(𝑔)𝑓)(𝑥)=𝑓(𝑔−1𝑥),(𝑓,𝑓′)=1|𝐺|∑𝑔∈𝐺𝑓(𝑔)𝑓′(𝑔)‾. (11)

Then (𝜆,𝐹(𝐺)) is a unitary representation.

Lemma 4 (All finite representations are unitary). Let (𝜌,𝑉) be a finite-dimensional representation of 𝐺 over a field with characteristic not dividing |𝐺|. Then (𝜌,𝑉) is unitary.
Proof of Lemma 3

It suffices to find a 𝐺-invariant positive definite Hermitian form. Let (,) be an arbitrary positive definite Hermitian product. Construct a new positive definite Hermitian product as

⟨𝑣,𝑤⟩=1|𝐺|∑𝑔∈𝐺(𝑔𝑣,𝑔𝑤). (12)
Clearly (𝜌,𝑉) is unitary with respect to ⟨,⟩. □

Remark. If the field has characteristic dividing |𝐺|, the the resulting formula is not a valid inner product: ⟨𝑣,𝑣⟩=0 for all 𝑣.

Lemma 5. Suppose (𝜌,𝑉) is unitary and 𝑊 is 𝐺-invariant. Then 𝑊⟂ is also 𝐺-invariant.

(𝑊⟂ is defined using the inner product that makes 𝜌 unitary.)

Proof of Lemma 4

Suppose 𝑢∈𝑊⟂. Then for all 𝑤 and 𝑔 we have

⟨𝑤,𝜌(𝑔)𝑢⟩=⟨𝜌(𝑔)†𝑤,𝑢⟩=⟨𝜌(𝑔−1)𝑤,𝑢⟩=0 (13)
Proof of Maschke's Theorem
Suppose (𝜌,𝑉) is not irreducible. Then there exists a nonproper 𝐺-invariant 𝑊. Thus we can express 𝜌 as a direct sum with respect to 𝑊 and 𝑊⟂. Then we can apply strong induction2 (base case: any 1-dimensional representation is irreducible)

Remark. A stronger form of the theorem states that (𝜌,𝑉) is completely reducible if and only if the characteristic of 𝔽 does not divide |𝐺|.

Corollary. Suppose 𝐺 is finite abelian. Since commuting unitary operators have a common eigenbasis, every irreducible representation of 𝐺 is 1-dimensional.

We will see another way to prove the above corollary with the Main Theorem.

1.7. Extension to compact groups

For compact topological groups, the same architecture survives almost verbaitim verbatim once you require 𝜌 to be continuousand replace the average by integation against Haar measure.

  • A representation is now a continuous homomorphism into GL(𝑉)
  • Schur’s lemma is unchanged for finite-dimensional irreduciible complex representations.
  • Every finite-dimensional continuous representatino is still unitarizable. Start with any Hermtian form and average it over 𝐺: ⟨𝑣,𝑤⟩=∫𝐺(𝜌(𝑔)𝑣,𝜌(𝑔)𝑤)d𝑔
  • Hence finite-dmensional representations of compact groups are completely reducible
