A sequence of fair games shouldn’t lead to an unfair outcome. Let’s try to formalize this.

Definition 2. A discrete stochastic process (𝑀𝑡)𝑡∈ℕ is a martingale w.r.t. a filtration (ℱ︀𝑡) if

  1. 𝑀𝑡 is ℱ︀𝑡-measurable. That is, the value of 𝑀𝑡 can be determined given everything you know up to time 𝑡 inclusive.
  2. 𝔼[|𝑀𝑡|]<∞
  3. 𝔼[𝑀𝑡+1|ℱ︀𝑡]=𝑀𝑡,∀𝑡.

A submartingale/supermartingale replaces condition 3 with ≥ and ≤, respectively. Note, “sub” means “increasing” here!

Note. The one-step martingale property (#3), automatically implies for fixed deterministic times 𝑠<𝑡, 𝔼[𝑀𝑡|ℱ︀𝑠]=𝑀𝑠 by applying the tower law repeatedly.

Examples:

  1. Random walk: 𝑀𝑡=𝑋1+…+𝑋𝑡, where 𝑋𝑖 are i.i.d. with mean 0, and ℱ︀𝑡=𝜎(𝑋1,…,𝑋𝑡).

  2. Cumulative coin flips: 𝑋𝑖∼Bernoulli(1/2),

    𝑀𝑡=∑𝑖=1𝑡(𝑋𝑖−12)=#{heads up to time𝑡}−𝑡2. (20)
  3. Squared random walk is strictly a submartingale: 𝐸[𝑀𝑡+12|ℱ︀𝑡]=𝑀𝑡2+1.
  4. Let 𝑋 be an indicator variable for whether you win the game. Define 𝑝(𝑡) as 𝔼[𝑋|𝐹𝑡]. Then 𝑝(𝑡) is a martingale by the tower law.

Proposition 6. Let 𝑆𝑡 be the position of ±1 random walk. Then 𝑀𝑡=𝑆𝑡2−𝑡 is also a martingale. More generally, let Δ𝑀𝑛≔𝑀𝑛−𝑀𝑛−1. Then the following is also a martingale:

𝑀𝑛2−∑𝑘=1𝑛𝔼[(Δ𝑀𝑘)2|ℱ︀𝑘−1]. (21)

This allows us to compute the variance of 𝑀𝜏 or the expectation of the stopping time if we know the other (See after Example 5).

1.4.1. Stopping Times

Definition 3. A random time 𝜏 is a stopping time (wrt (ℱ︀𝑡)) if the event 𝜏≤𝑡 can be decided using only info up to time 𝑡.

Examples include: first time you hit +𝑎 in a random walk or leave the interval [−𝑏,𝑎].

Theorem 3 (Optional Stopping Theorem). Let (𝑀𝑡) be a martingale, 𝜏 a stopping time. Under mild conditions,

𝔼[𝑀𝜏]=𝔼[𝑀0]. (22)

One of the three following conditions must hold

  1. 𝜏 is bounded a.s.
  2. 𝔼[𝜏]<∞ and increments are bounded.
  3. (𝑀min(𝑡,𝜏)) is uniformly integrable, i.e. the martingale cannot accumulate arbitrarily large expectation from tails as 𝑡 grows.
Proof

The OST is indeed conveying something nontrivial. Unlike the tower law statement above, 𝜏 need not be deterministic.

  • For bounded stopping times it’s just the tower law.
  • Unbounded stopping times with finite expectation (e.g. 𝜏∼Geom(𝑝)), which is unbounded but has finite expectation. Proof requires dominated convergence.

□

Informally, the OST says that you can’t do anything at the beginning of a fair game to bias your probability of winning / winnings. In the doubling cube game that Alex showed me, if you start with the cube, the dice has value so you’re expected winnings are positive.

An example where OST doesn’t apply: random walk, let 𝜏 be the first time we hit +1, and let 𝑆𝑛 be your position. 𝔼[𝑆𝜏]=1≠0=𝔼[𝑆0]. The reason OST doesn’t hold is because 𝔼[𝜏]=∞ (check using telescoping catalan sum that 𝑃(𝜏>2𝑛)∼1𝜋𝑛 and use stirlings formula with third term).

1.4.2. Examples

Example 5 (Random walk with absorption). Suppose we keep playing a random walk until we either win 𝑎 dollars or lose 𝑏 dollars. Since the martingale is bounded from both sides, it is uniformly integratable and the OST applys:

𝔼[𝑆𝜏]=𝔼[𝑆0]=0⟹𝑃(𝑆𝜏=𝑎)=𝑏𝑎+𝑏. (23)

This holds for general martingales, not just random walks!

As a consistency check that 𝔼[𝜏]is bounded, consider the martingale 𝑀𝑡≔𝑆𝑡2−𝑡, and apply optional stopping. 𝔼[𝜏]=𝔼[𝑆𝜏2]≤max(𝑎,𝑏)2.

Once we’ve proven 𝜏 is bounded, we can use the above results to compute its expectation:

𝔼[𝜏]=𝔼[𝑆𝜏2]=𝑏𝑎+𝑏𝑎2+𝑎𝑎+𝑏𝑏2=𝑎𝑏. (24)

The proof without martingales would have required solving a recurrence.

Example 6 (Biased random walk). Check that 𝑀𝑡≔𝑟𝑆𝑡 is a martingale where 𝑟=𝑞𝑝. Then

𝑃(𝑆𝜏=𝑎)=𝑟𝑏−1𝑟𝑎+𝑏−1. (25)
The fair case emerges from applying L Hopital.

We can also center the biased random walk to get another martingale: 𝑆𝑡−(2𝑝−1)𝑡. This is useful for computing expectation of stopping time after we used the other martingale to compute absorption properties.

1.5. Brownian Motion

Definition 4. A standard Brownian motion (𝐵𝑡)𝑡≥0 is a continuous time stochastic process, where

  1. 𝐵0=0
  2. For 0≤𝑠<𝑡, the increment 𝐵𝑡−𝐵𝑠 is independent of the past ℱ︀𝑠.
  3. 𝐵𝑡−𝐵𝑠∼𝒩︀(0,𝑡−𝑠) (linearly increasing variance).
  4. 𝑡↦𝐵𝑡 is almost surely continuous.

Brownian motion is a specific type of Gaussian process with Cov(𝐵𝑠,𝐵𝑡)=Var(𝐵𝑠∧𝑡). Intuition: time between 𝑠 and 𝑡 is random noise that doesn’t affect the covariance. Up until time 𝑠, they have the same noise history. Note that Var(B_s - B_t)= |t-s|.

Definition 5 (Continuous-time martingale). A process (𝑋𝑡,ℱ︀𝑡)𝑡≥0 is a martingale if

  1. 𝑋𝑡 is ℱ︀𝑡-measurable.
  2. 𝔼[|𝑋𝑡|]<∞ for all 𝑡.
  3. For all 𝑠<𝑡, 𝔼[𝑋𝑡|ℱ︀𝑠]=𝑋𝑠.

This is a stronger requirement than the discrete case.

  • By definition, Brownian motion is a martingale.
  • Again by the same reasoning as the discrete case, 𝐵𝑡2−𝑡 is also a martingale.
  • |𝐵𝑡| is a submartingale by Jensen’s inequality: 𝐸⟦𝐵𝑡+ℎ||ℱ︀𝑡]≥|𝔼[𝐵𝑡+ℎ|ℱ︀𝑡]|=|𝐵𝑡|.
  • 𝑒𝜆𝐵𝑡 is a submartingale by Jensen’s inequality.
