6. Bilinear Forms

6.1. Definition

  • Def A bilinear form on 𝑉 is a map 𝐵:𝑉×𝑉→𝔽, such that 𝐵(𝑥,𝑦) is linear in 𝑦 for fixed 𝑥 and linear in 𝑥 for fixed 𝑦.
    • 𝐵 is called symmetric/skew-symmetric if 𝐵(𝑥,𝑦)=±𝐵(𝑦,𝑥) respectively for all 𝑥,𝑦∈𝑉.

    • Prop (Matrix representation)
      Let 𝐁={𝑒𝑖} be a basis of 𝑉, and 𝑥=𝐁𝑋=∑𝑖𝑥𝑖𝑒𝑖 and 𝑦=𝐁𝑌=∑𝑗𝑦𝑗𝑒𝑗. Then

      𝐵(𝑥,𝑦)=∑𝑖𝑗𝑥𝑖𝑦𝑗𝐵(𝑒𝑖,𝑒𝑗)=𝑋𝖳[𝐵]𝑌 (48)

      where the matrix of 𝐵 in the basis {𝑒𝑖} is given by [𝐵]𝑖𝑗=𝐵(𝑒𝑖,𝑒𝑗).

    • Prop (Change of basis15)
      Suppose 𝐁′=𝐁𝑃 so that 𝑋=𝑃𝑋′,𝑌=𝑃𝑌′. Then the matrix of 𝐵 with respect to the new basis is

      [𝐵]′=𝑃𝖳[𝐵]𝑃. (49)
    • Note that the determinant of a bilinear form is not well-defined since det(𝐵′)=det(𝐵)det(𝑃)2. However, it’s sign is.

6.2. Symmetric forms

  • Suppose we are given a symmetric/Hermitian form ⟨,⟩ over a finite-dimensional real/complex space.
  • Def An orthogonal basis {𝑒𝑖} of 𝑉 satisfies ⟨𝑒𝑖,𝑒𝑗⟩=0 for all 𝑖≠𝑗.
    • Prop ⟨,⟩ is nondegenerate if and only if ⟨𝑒𝑖,𝑒𝑖⟩≠0 for all 𝑖, i.e. [𝐵] has nonzero diagonal entries.

  • Def The orthogonal space to a subspace 𝑊 of 𝑉 is

    𝑊⟂={𝑣∈𝑉|⟨𝑣,𝑤⟩=0for all𝑤∈𝑊}. (50)

  • Def 𝐵 is nondegenerate if ker𝐵=0 i.e. for all nonzero 𝑥∈𝑉, there exists 𝑦∈𝑉 such that 𝐵(𝑥,𝑦)≠0. 𝐵 is nondegenerate if and only if [𝐵] is invertible.

    • Def Suppose 𝑊 is a subspace of 𝑉. A form on 𝑉 is nondegenerate on 𝑊 if its restriction to 𝑊 is a nondegenerate form.
    • Ex A form may be degenerate on a subspace, though it is nondegenerate on the whole space and vice versa. For example, consider
    𝑉=ℝ2, [𝐵]=(01−10), 𝑊=span{(10)} (51)
    • Extend the kernel of a degenerate form 𝐵 to a basis of 𝑉. Then the matrix of 𝐵 with respect to this basis is [𝐵]=(𝐵‾000), so it remains to classify nondegenerate bilinear forms.

  • Lem 1. Suppose 𝑊 is a subspace of 𝑉. The following statements are equivalent:

    1. A symmetric form is nondegenerate on 𝑊.
    2. 𝑊∩𝑊⟂={0}.
    3. 𝑊⊕𝑊⟂=𝑉

    Proof. (1) ↔ (2) is easy. We cannot use orthogonal projection to prove (3) because we not know if an orthogonal basis exists (proved below). Instead consider applying a change of basis 𝑃=(𝐼𝑄0𝐼) to 𝐵, and use the fact that 𝐵′ remains symmetric.

  • Lem 2. Let ⟨,⟩ be a nondegenerate symmetric or Hermitian form on 𝑉, and let 𝑣,𝑣′∈𝑉. If ⟨𝑣,𝑤⟩=⟨𝑣′,𝑤⟩ for all 𝑤∈𝑉, then 𝑣=𝑣′.

  • Lem 3. If a symmetric/Hermitian form is not identically zero, there is a vector in 𝑉 such that ⟨𝑣,𝑣⟩=0.

    Proof. For a symmetric form, ⟨𝑣,𝑤⟩=⟨𝑣+𝑤,𝑣+𝑤⟩−⟨𝑣,𝑣⟩−⟨𝑤,𝑤⟩2, so if the LHS is nonzero one of the vectors on the RHS must be nonzero. For a Hermitian form, replace 𝑤 by 𝑐𝑤 to make ⟨𝑣,𝑤⟩ real first.

  • Thm Every symmetric bilinear form, degenerate or nondegenerate, admits an orthogonal basis.

    Proof.
    Suppose ⟨,⟩ is identically zero. Then every bases is orthogonal.

    Suppose ⟨,⟩ is not identically zero. Proceed by induction on 𝑛=dim𝑉. 𝑛=1 is trivially true. By Lem 3 there exists a vector 𝑣 such that ⟨𝑣,𝑣⟩≠0. Let 𝑊=span{𝑣}. The form is clearly nondegenerate on 𝑊, therefore 𝑉=𝑊⊕𝑊⟂ by Lem 1. Apply the induction hypothesis to 𝑊⟂.

  • Cor For nondegenerate symmetric forms we can apply orthogonal projection.
    • [∗] Prop Orthogonal projection is a symmetric operator.
  • Cor A symmetric form on a f.d. vector space admits an orthogonal basis in which [𝐵] is a diagonal matrix with 1s, −1s, and 0s on the diagonal.
  • Def Let 𝑉 be a real vector space. A symmetric form on 𝑉 is:
    • Positive definite if ⟨𝑣,𝑣⟩>0 for all nonzero 𝑣.
    • Positive semidefinite if ⟨𝑣,𝑣⟩≥0 for all nonzero 𝑣.
    • Indefinite if not ⟨,⟩ is not positive definite.
  • Prop (Sylvester Criterion) 𝐵 is positive definite if and only if the 𝑗×𝑗 submatrices (called corner matrices) have positive determinants for all 𝑗=1,..,𝑛.
    • The same result does not hold for checking for positive semidefiniteness. We instead need to verify all principle minors of 𝑀 are nonnegative, not just the leading ones.

6.3. Hermitian forms

  • Def Let 𝑉 be a vector space over ℂ.
    • A semilinear form 𝑓:𝑉→ℂ satisfies 𝑓(𝑥+𝑦)=𝑓(𝑥)+𝑓(𝑦) and 𝑓(𝜆𝑥)=𝜆‾𝑓(𝑥).
    • A sesquilinear form 𝐵:𝑉×𝑉→ℂ is semilinear in the first splot and linear in the second slot.
    • A Hermitian form is a sesquilinear form satisfying Hermiticity: 𝐵(𝑥,𝑦)=𝐵(𝑦,𝑥)‾.
      • Prop [𝐵] equals its conjugate transpose [𝐵]∗.
      • Prop The space of Hermitian forms forms an 𝑛2 dimensional real vector space (subtle!).
    • Def (Inner product space) A Euclidean/Hermitian space is a real/complex vector space equipped with a positive definite symmetric/Hermitian form.
  • Prop Everything about symmetric operators from last section carries over for Hermitian forms.

6.4. Signature of a symmetric form

  • Recall by existence of orthogonal basis for symmetric/Hermitian forms, we could obtain a diagonal matrix for a form with 𝑝 1s, 𝑚 −1s, and some 0s.
  • Def The signature of a nondegenerate form is the tuple (𝑝,𝑚).

  • Thm (Sylvester) When 𝐹=ℝ, The signature does not depend on choice of basis.

    Proof. Suppose ⟨,⟩ has two orthogonal bases with signatures (𝑝1,𝑚1) and (𝑝2,𝑚2) such that 𝑝1+𝑚1=𝑝2+𝑚2=𝑛 with 𝑝1>𝑝2. Let 𝑊1 be spanned by the 𝑝1 basis and 𝑊2 be spanned by the 𝑚2 basis. Note that 𝑊1∩𝑊2={0} since we cannot have a vector 𝑤∈𝑉 such that ⟨𝑤,𝑤⟩ is both positive and negative. Thus 𝑊1⊕𝑊2 is a subspace of 𝑉, but the left hand side has dimension greater than dim𝑉, yielding a contradiction. Thus we must have 𝑝1=𝑝2.

    • Cor There are 𝑛+1 equivalence classes of nondegenerate symmetric forms in an 𝑛-dimensional vector space.
  • Ex For other fields, the signature is not necessarily invariant under change of basis! Consider 𝐹=𝔽3 and 𝑉=𝐹2. The quadratic forms 𝑄1(𝑥𝑦)=𝑥2+𝑦2 and 𝑄2(𝑥𝑦)=−𝑥2−𝑦2 are related by the change of basis 𝑥=𝑥′+𝑦′ and 𝑦=𝑥′−𝑦′. Thus the parity of (𝑝,𝑚) is conserved.

6.5. Quadratic forms

  • Def A quadratic form on 𝑉 is a function 𝑄:𝑉→𝔽 such that
    1. 𝑄 is even: 𝑄(𝑣)=𝑄(−𝑣).
    2. 𝐵(𝑣,𝑤)≝𝑄(𝑣+𝑤)−𝑄(𝑣)−𝑄(𝑤) is a (manifestly symmetric) bilinear form on 𝑉.
  • Prop
    1. 𝑄(0)=0
    2. There is a bijection between quadratic forms and symmetric bilinear forms, in particular 𝑄(𝑥)=12𝐵(𝑥,𝑥) (Consider 𝐵(𝑥,−𝑥)=−𝐵(𝑥,𝑥))
    3. 𝑄(𝑥)=𝑥𝖳𝐵𝑥=12∑𝑖𝑛𝐵𝑖𝑖𝑥𝑖2+∑𝑖<𝑗𝑏𝑖𝑗𝑥𝑖𝑥𝑗.
  • Ex The quadratic form corresponding to the standard inner product of ℝ𝑛 is 𝑄(𝑥)=‖𝑥‖2.

6.6. Skew-symmetric forms

  • Lem If 𝐵 is skew symmetric and 𝐵(𝑒𝑖,𝑓𝑖)≠0 then 𝑒𝑖 and 𝑓𝑖 are linearly independent.
  • Thm Every 2𝑛×2𝑛 nondegenerate skew-symmetric form admits a basis {𝑒1,𝑓1,𝑒2,𝑓2,…,𝑒𝑛,𝑓𝑛} called the sympletic basis such that [𝐵] is a block diagonal matrix with (01−10) as the 𝑛 blocks.

6.7. Adjoint and Normal Operators

  • Let 𝑉 be a Euclidean or Hermitian space, and 𝐴 be a linear operator.

  • Prop (Riesz representation theorem)
    There exists a unique operator 𝐴† (called the adjoint) such that

    ⟨𝐴†𝑥,𝑦⟩=⟨𝑥,𝐴𝑦⟩∀𝑥,𝑦∈𝑉. (52)

    Proof. There exists a basis where [𝐵]=𝐼. Work under this basis. Then

    𝐵(𝐴𝑥,𝑦)=𝑋𝖳𝐴𝖳𝐵𝑌=𝑋𝖳𝐵𝐴𝖳𝑌 (53)
    since 𝐵 and 𝐴𝖳 commute. Thus 𝐴∗=𝐴𝖳 for a Euclidean space, and 𝐴†=𝐴∗ for a Hermitian space. To prove uniqueness, use Lem 2 from 6.2.
  • Def A matrix is self-adjoint or Hermitian if 𝐴=𝐴∗.

    • Prop The eigenvalues of a self-adjoint matrix are real.
      Proof. For an eigenvector 𝑣, ⟨𝑣,𝐴𝑣⟩=⟨𝐴𝑣,𝑣⟩, implying 𝜆=𝜆‾ since ⟨𝑣,𝑣⟩ is nonzero.

    • Cor The eigenvalues of a real symmetric matrix are real.
  • Def 𝐴 is normal if 𝐴𝐴†=𝐴†𝐴.
    • Ex Self-adjoint operators are normal.

    • Lem If 𝐴 is normal, then

      1. ‖𝐴𝑣‖=‖𝐴∗𝑣‖ for all 𝑣∈𝑉.
      2. 𝐴𝑣=𝜆𝑣 implies 𝐴∗𝑣=𝜆‾𝑣.
      3. Eigenvectors of 𝐴 corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.

6.8. Spectral Theorem

  • [∗] Lem 1. Suppose 𝐴 is a symmetric matrix. Then 𝐴 has an eigenvalue.

    Proof. Take any nonzero 𝑣∈𝑉, and consider the list {𝑣,𝐴𝑣,𝐴2𝑣,…,𝐴𝑛𝑣}. This is a list of 𝑛+1 vectors, so it must be linearly dependent, i.e. there exist 𝑐0,…,𝑐𝑛−1∈ℝ such that

    𝐴𝑛𝑣+𝑐𝑛−1𝐴𝑛−1𝑣+…+𝑐1𝐴𝑣+𝑐0𝑣=0 (54)

    Factoring the equation and rearranging yields

    [∏𝑖(𝐴2+𝑏𝑖𝐴+𝑐𝑖𝐼)][∏𝑗(𝐴−𝜆𝑖𝐼)]𝑣=0. (55)

    Each quadratic in the left product corresponds to two conjugate complex roots (𝑏𝑖2<4𝑐𝑖), and each term in the right corresponds to a real root. Each quadratic must be injective (prove it), so we must have at least one term in the right product that is not injective. Thus 𝐴 has an eigenvalue.

  • Thm (Real spectral theorem) Suppose 𝑉 is a f.d. Euclidean space, and let 𝐴 be a symmetric operator. Then 𝐴 admits an orthonormal eigenbasis.

    Proof. Take an eigenvector 𝑣1 corresponding to eigenvalue 𝜆1 (guaranteed to exist by Lem 1), and let 𝑊=span{𝑣1}. Since 𝑉=𝑊⊕𝑊⟂ , we have the matrix of 𝐴 with respect to the basis is

    𝐴∼(𝜆1∗0𝐴′) (56)

    Suppose 𝑢∈𝑊⟂. ⟨𝐴𝑢,𝑣1⟩=⟨𝑢,𝐴𝑣1⟩=0. Thus 𝑊⟂ is invariant under 𝐴, and the asterisk region is zero. Alternatively we could have taken an orthonormal change of basis and used the fact that 𝐴=𝐴𝖳. Apply the induction hypothesis to 𝐴′=𝐴|𝑊⟂. □

  • Let 𝑉 be a f.d. Hermitian space, and let {𝐴𝑖} be a family of self-adjoint operators that mutually commute with each other: [𝐴𝑖,𝐴𝑗]=0 ∀𝑖,𝑗∈𝐼.

  • Thm (Commuting observables in QM) The family {𝐴𝑖} admits a common orthonormal eigenbasis.

    Proof. Induction on 𝑛=dim𝑉. 𝑛=1 is trivial. Take eigenvector 𝑣 of 𝐴𝑖 in the eigenspace 𝑉𝑖(𝜆) . Then 𝐴𝑖𝐴𝑗𝑣=𝐴𝑗𝐴𝑖𝑣, implying 𝑉𝑖(𝜆) is invariant under each 𝐴𝑗.

    Each 𝑉𝑖(𝜆) has an common orthonormal eigenbasis by the inductive hypothesis. Stitch them together, and we’re done (We are guaranteed that 𝑉𝑖(𝜆) are orthogonal). Edge case: If all {𝐴𝑖} are scalar opeartors, then any orthonormal basis works. So we picked above 𝐴𝑖 with a nontrivial orthogonal decomposition. □

  • Lem 2. Suppose 𝑉 is a Hermitian space. If 𝑊 is 𝐴-invariant, then 𝑊⟂ is 𝐴∗ invariant.

  • Thm (Complex Spectral Theorem) Suppose 𝑉 is a f.d. Hermitian space, and let 𝐴 be a normal operator. Then 𝐴 admits an orthonormal eigenbasis.

    Proof 1. Induction on 𝑛=dim𝑉. 𝑛=1 is trivial. Take any eigenvalue 𝜆. Then 𝑉=𝑉(𝜆)⊕𝑉(𝜆)⟂. Since 𝐴 and 𝐴∗ commute, 𝑉(𝜆) is invariant under 𝐴∗, 𝑉(𝜆)⟂ is invariant under 𝐴 by Lem 2. Apply the inductive hypothesis.

    Proof 2. Let 𝐵=Re𝐴=𝐴+𝐴∗2 and 𝐶=Im𝐴=𝐴−𝐴∗2. 𝐵 and 𝐶 commute and are both self-adjoint, so we have a common orthonormal eigenbasis. Furthermore, 𝐴=𝐵+𝑖𝐶 so the eigenvalues of 𝐴 are in the form 𝜆𝐴=𝜆𝐵+𝑖𝜆𝐶. □

    Cor Every unitary operator (𝑈𝑈†=𝐼) has an orthonormal eigenbasis with eigenvalues |𝜆𝑖|=1.

    Proof. 𝑇𝑣=𝜆𝑣 and 𝑇†𝑣=𝑇−1𝑣=𝜆−1𝑣=𝜆‾𝑣. This implies |𝜆|=1.

6.9. Gram–Schmidt and QR decomposition

  • Prop (Gram-Schmidt) Any basis in a Euclidean/Hermitian space can be made orthonormal.

  • Ex Let 𝑉=ℂ[𝑥] and 𝑤(𝑥) be a positive integratable function on ℝ which decays rapidly at ∞ (faster than any polynomial, but 𝑤≠0). 𝑤 is called a weight function. We have a positive-definite Hermitian form defined by

    ⟨𝑓,𝑔⟩=∫−∞∞𝑤(𝑥)𝑓(𝑥)‾𝑔(𝑥)d𝑥. (57)

    Let {𝑥𝑖,𝑖=0,1,2,…} be the basis of monomials. Then Gram-Schmidt yields:

    𝑤(𝑥)={1|𝑥|<10otherwise⟹Legendre polynomials𝑤(𝑥)={2𝜋11−𝑥2|𝑥|<10otherwise⟹Chebyshev polynomials16𝑤(𝑥)=𝑒−𝑥2⟹Hermite polynomials𝑤(𝑥)={𝑒−𝑥2𝑥≥00otherwise⟹Laguerre polynomials (58)

  • Prop (𝑄𝑅 decomposition) Every invertible complex matrix 𝐵 can be uniquely written as 𝑄𝑅 where 𝑄 is unitary and 𝑅 is upper triangular with positive diagonal.

    Proof. Take columns and apply G.S. to show existence. Suppose 𝐵=𝑄1𝑅1=𝑄2𝑅2. Then 𝑄2−1𝑄1=𝑅2𝑅1−1 is both unitary and upper triangular, so it must be the identity.

6.11. Polar decomposition

  • Thm (Polar Decomposition)
    Every invertible complex matrix 𝐴 can be uniquely written as 𝐴=𝑈𝑃 where 𝑈 is unitary and 𝑃 is positive. In particular, 𝑃=𝐴†𝐴.
    • If 𝐴 is not invertible, 𝑃 is self-adjoint but not necessarily positive, and there are multiple allowed values for 𝑈, in particular, 𝑈 is uniquely defined on the orthogonal complement of ker𝐴, and can be extended nonuniquely to the entier space to remain unitary.
    • Geometrically, the polar composition can be interpretted as a rotation plus a scaling along 𝑛 orthogonal axes.
