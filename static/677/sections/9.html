9. Stochastic Processes

Definition 21. A discrete-time stochastic process is a sequence of random variables {𝑋𝑛} defined on a common probability space (Ω,ℱ︀,ℙ).

A continuous-time stochastic process is defined similarly, as a collection of random variables {𝑋𝑡} where 𝑡 varies over the nonnegative real values ℝ+.

9.1. The Bernoulli Process

In the Bernoulli process, the random variables 𝑋𝑛 are iid Bernoulli random variables with a common parameter 𝑝∈(0,1). The natural sample space is Ω={0,1}𝑛.

The Bernoulli process satisfies

9.1.1. Stopping Times

Consider the notion of starting to watch later. Let 𝑁 be a random variable. Is {𝑋𝑁+𝑛} a Bernoulli process with the same parameter as {𝑋𝑛}? Not necessarily, for example define 𝑁=min{𝑥|𝑋𝑛+1=1}. This motivates us to consider 𝑁 that are causal, i.e. stopping times.

Proposition 13 (Memorylessness). If 𝑁 is a stopping time, i.e. 𝐼{𝑁=𝑛}=ℎ𝑛(𝑋1,…,𝑋𝑛) for some function ℎ𝑛, then for all 𝑛 we have

ℙ((𝑥𝑁+1,𝑋𝑁+2,…)∈𝐴|𝑁=𝑛,𝑋1,…,𝑋𝑛)=ℙ((𝑋1,𝑋2,…)∈𝐴). (67)
I.e if we start watching the Bernoulli process right after a stopping time, it follows a Bernoulli process as well.

9.1.2. Arrival Times

The interarrival times (difference in arrival times) are i.i.d. geometric. The probability that the 𝑘th arrival occurs at 𝑡 is then

ℙ(𝑌𝑘=𝑡)=ℙ(𝑆𝑡−1=𝑘−1)⋅ℙ(𝑋𝑡=1). (68)

9.1.3. Merging and splitting

Merging: Let {𝑋𝑛} and {𝑌𝑛} be independent Bernoulli processes with parameters 𝑝 and 𝑞, respectively. Consider a “merged” process {𝑍𝑛} defined by 𝑍𝑛=max{𝑋𝑛,𝑌𝑛}. Then {𝑍𝑛} is a Bernoulli process with parameter 1−(1−𝑝)(1−𝑞.

Splitting: If there is an arrival at time 𝑛, flip an independent coin with parameter 𝑞 and record an arrival in either 𝑋𝑛 or 𝑌𝑛. This yields two dependent processes: 𝑋𝑛 and 𝑌𝑛 cannot be simultaneously one.

The Poisson process intuitively is a continuous time analog of the Bernoulli process.
