9. Identical Particles

9.1. Need for Symmetrization

Definition 11. Two particles are said to be identical if no experiment can distinguish them. Identical particles share intrinsic properties such as mass, charge, spin, and magnetic moments.

Examples: all electrons are identical, and all protons are identical.

For two identical particles, the classical Hamiltonian should be invariant under exchanging labels:

𝐻(𝐱1,𝐩1;𝐱2;𝐩2)=𝐻(𝐱2,𝐩2;𝐱1;𝐩1). (119)

Symmetrization Postulate

In a system with 𝑁 identical particles, the states that are physically realized are not arbitrary states in 𝑉⊗𝑁 but rather are totally symmetric (𝑁 identical bosons) are totally antisymmetric (𝑁 identical fermions). That is, the states must belong to Sym𝑁𝑉 or Anti𝑁𝑉.

This postulate solves the exchange degeneracy problem by the following claim:

Proposition 19 (Uniqueness). Let |𝑢⟩∈𝑉⊗𝑛 be an arbitrary state, and let 𝑉|𝑢⟩ be the subspace spanned by the orbit of 𝑆𝑛acting on |𝑢⟩. Up to a multiplicative constant, 𝑉|𝑢⟩ contains at most a single state in Sym𝑁𝑉 and at most a single state in Anti𝑁𝑉. That is, any state |𝑢⟩ uniquely corresponds to a boson and fermion if they exist.
Proof
Suppose |𝜓⟩∈𝑉|𝑢⟩ is symmetric. Write |Ψ⟩ as a sum over the orbit of |𝑢⟩. Use the fact that it remains constant under 𝑆/𝐴, and use Lemma 3, and show that all such states are scalar multiples of 𝑆|𝑢⟩ and 𝐴|𝑢⟩.

□

Thus by uniqueaness the bosonic state is 𝑆|𝑢⟩ and the fermion state is 𝐴|𝑢⟩. It may be the case that 𝑆|𝑢⟩=0, meaning that there is no bosonic state. Some useful conditions are the following:

Proposition 20 (No Boson). If |𝑢⟩ satisfies 𝑃𝛼|𝑢⟩=−|𝑢⟩ for some permutation, then 𝑆|𝑢⟩=0.
Proof
Multiply both sides by 𝑆 and use the fact that 𝑆𝑃𝛼=𝑆.

□

Proposition 21 (No Fermion aka Pauli Exclusion Principle). If two or more of the 𝑉 states in |𝑢⟩ appearing in |𝑢⟩ are the same, then the state Anti𝑁𝑉 will fail to exist in 𝑉|𝑢⟩. This means that two or more identical fermions cannot be found in the same (eigen)state (wrt some complete basis).
Proof
There exists a transposition (𝑝𝑞) that leaves |𝑢⟩ invariant. Then 𝐴(𝑝𝑞)𝑢=−𝐴|𝑢⟩, implying 𝐴|𝑢⟩−𝐴|𝑢⟩=0. No state exists.

□

Remarks

  1. Bosons and fermions have strikingly different statistical behavior.
  2. Quantum field theory plus special relativity can be used to prove the spin-statistics theorem, which shows that bosons and fermions have integer and half-integer spin, respectively.
  3. Composite particles made are also either bosons or fermions. For example, consider two hydrogen atoms, with wavefunction 𝜓(𝑝1,𝑒1,𝑝2,𝑒2). Since protons and electrons are fermions, exchanging them both implies the hydrogen atom is a boson!
  4. “The postulate is in fact a description of the most general statistics of particles that live in three spatial dimensions. A closer look into the physics requires thinking of permutation-induced exchanges as physical processes in which the particles actually move in some trajectories to realize the permutations. It turns out on close examination that in three spatial dimensions there are no new possibilities, just bosons or fermions. But when space is two-dimensional, there are new possibilities. Transpositions of particles need not produce only sign factors on the wave function; general phases are in principle possible. Particles with such behavior are called anyons. We will discuss such phases at the end of this section.”

We must ensure the time evolution induced by the Hamitonian for a state in Sym𝑁𝑉 or in Anti𝑁𝑉 guarantees the state remains in Sym𝑁𝑉 or in Anti𝑁𝑉 at all times.

Let Θ(1,…,𝑁) be constructed in terms of arbitrary sets of opertaors acting on the various particles, e.g adding and composing operators in the form 𝐵𝑘=𝟙⊗…⊗𝐵⊗…𝟙. Then

𝑃𝜎†Θ(1,…,𝑁)𝑃𝜎=Θ(𝜎(1),…,𝜎(𝑁)). (126)

Definition 13. A Hermitian operator 𝑀̂(1,2,…,𝑁) is said to be a completely symmetric observable if 𝑀 is invariant under permutations, i.e.

𝑀(𝜎(1),…,𝜎(𝑁))=𝑀(1,…,𝑁). (127)

𝐴|𝑢⟩=13!||𝜔1⟩(1)|𝜔1⟩(2)|𝜔1⟩(3)|𝜔2⟩(1)|𝜔2⟩(2)|𝜔2⟩(3)|𝜔3⟩(1)|𝜔3⟩(2)|𝜔3⟩(3)| (128)

9.5. Occupation Numbers

Many different basis states in 𝑉⊗𝑁 lead to the same symmetric/antisymmetric states when applying 𝑆 or 𝐴. The solution is to define occupation numbers to distinguish states in 𝑉⊗𝑁 that are linearly independent after applying 𝑆 or 𝐴.

Definition 14. Let {|𝑢𝑖⟩,𝑖=1,2,…} be an orthonormal basis of 𝑉 so that standard basis states in 𝑉⊗𝑁 take the form

|𝑢𝑖1⟩1⊗…⊗|𝑢𝑖𝑁⟩𝑁 (138)

For each standard basis state |𝜔⟩∈𝑉⊗𝑁, assign it to a set {𝑛1,𝑛2,…} of occupation numbers where 𝑛𝑖≥0 is the nmber of times |𝑢𝑖⟩ appears in |𝑤⟩. Since we are describing states of 𝑁 particles, the list of nonzero occupation numbers is finite (must sum to 𝑁).

For bosons, each occupation number may be any natural number. For fermions, each occupation number must be zero or one.

Example 9 (2 particles in 3 state system). We have 6 possible occupation numbers out of the 9 basis states. All 6 can be bosons, be only 3 can be fermions by Pauli exclusion.

States in Sym𝑁𝑉 and Anti𝑁𝑉 are uniquely characterized by occupation numbers, as applying a permutation operator on a standard basis state does not affect the occupation number. Write

|𝑛1,𝑛2,…⟩𝑆=𝑐𝑠𝑆̂(|𝑢1⟩⊗𝑛1⊗|𝑢1⟩⊗𝑛2⊗…)|𝑛1,𝑛2,…⟩𝑆=𝑐𝑠𝐴̂(|𝑢1⟩⊗𝑛1⊗|𝑢1⟩⊗𝑛2⊗…) (139)

so that

Sym𝑁𝑉=span{|𝑛1,𝑛2,…⟩𝑆|∑𝑖𝑛𝑖=𝑁,𝑛𝑖≥0}Anti𝑁𝑉=span{|𝑛1,𝑛2,…⟩𝐴|∑𝑖𝑛𝑖=𝑁,𝑛𝑖∈{0,1}} (140)

If there are a finite number of energy levels 𝑘, the dimensions of each space are, by combinatorics:

dimSym𝑁𝑉=(𝑁+𝑘−1)!𝑁!(𝑘−1)!dimAnti𝑁𝑉=(𝑘𝑁)𝑘≥𝑁 (141)

If 𝑘<𝑁, we cannot obtain any fermionic states. For example, we cannot bind more than two electrons to a delta function potential.

If using Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics (each particle is distinguishable), there are 𝑘𝑛 possible states.
